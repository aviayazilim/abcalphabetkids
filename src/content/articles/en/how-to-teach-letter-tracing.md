**In short:** start with a finger, not a pencil. Your child first meets the letter through its sound, then traces it with a finger, then follows the dotted line with a pencil, and finally writes it alone. Five to ten minutes a day is enough. The [letter tracing worksheets](/en/letter-tracing/) on this site follow exactly that order and are free.

## When is your child ready?

Age tells you little: some children trace letters at three and a half, others not until five. Look for these signs instead:

- holds the crayon with three fingers (thumb, index and middle) rather than a fist;
- can draw a straight line and a closed circle;
- can sit at a table activity for about 5 minutes;
- shows interest: asks “what letter is that?” or wants to write their name.

If those signs aren’t there yet, don’t push. Drawing, cutting with child scissors, building with blocks and squeezing play dough prepare the hand just as well as a worksheet.

## Before letters: pre-writing strokes

Every letter is made from a handful of strokes: vertical, horizontal and diagonal lines, circles and curves. Spend a week on “paths” before letter worksheets: lead the dog to its kennel with a straight line, draw waves on the sea, make circles for apples on a tree. Once the hand knows those movements, letters come much more easily.

## Step by step with one letter

1. **Meet the letter.** Say its sound, not just its name: “M for mouse, mmm.” Look for it on cereal boxes, signs and in picture books.
2. **Trace with a finger.** Your child runs a finger over the big letter, top to bottom, the way it is written. You can also draw it in the air, in sand, on a tray of salt or on a steamy window.
3. **Trace the dots.** Now with a pencil: capital first, then lowercase, then the word. A chunky triangular pencil is easier than a pen.
4. **Write alone.** The last line on the sheet is empty: your child writes the letter without dots, looking at the model.
5. **Color.** Finally, the coloring picture with the letter’s character is the reward, and it trains the same fine motor skills.

You don’t have to finish the whole sheet at once. Two careful letters are worth more than a full line written while tired.

## Which letters first?

For capitals, go from the simplest movement to the hardest:

| Stage | Letters | Why |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | [L](/en/letter-tracing/letter-l/), [T](/en/letter-tracing/letter-t/), [I](/en/letter-tracing/letter-i/), [E](/en/letter-tracing/letter-e/), F, H | straight lines only |
| 2 | [A](/en/letter-tracing/letter-a/), V, W, M, N, K, X, Y, Z | diagonal lines |
| 3 | [O](/en/letter-tracing/letter-o/), [C](/en/letter-tracing/letter-c/), Q, G, S | circles and curves, harder |
| 4 | B, D, P, R, U, J | lines and curves together |

Starting with the letters of your child’s name also works very well: they see them every day and have a real reason to write them.

## Uppercase first, then lowercase

Capitals all start at the top and use simple strokes, so many teachers introduce them first and add lowercase letters once the capitals feel easy. Lowercase letters are harder because several look alike (b, d, p, q). If your child’s preschool or kindergarten follows another order, go with the school so your child isn’t learning two ways at once.

## Stroke direction

What matters most isn’t that the letter looks pretty, but that it’s formed in the right direction: **top to bottom and left to right**. A letter drawn “from the bottom up” may look similar, but it slows down fluent writing later. Show the direction with your finger before your child picks up the pencil.

## Mistakes that are normal

- **Backwards letters** (a reversed S) and b/d mix-ups: common until about 6 or 7.
- **Going outside the dots** at first: the hand is still learning where to stop.
- **Letters of different sizes:** this settles with time and the guide lines on the sheet.

Don’t correct everything. Praise what worked (“that L is standing nice and straight!”) and trace the tricky letter together, your hand over theirs.

## How often to practice

Three or four sheets a week is plenty. At that pace you’ll finish the alphabet in two to three months, without pressure. Come back to letters that get mixed up (b, d, p, q) and the ones that felt hard.
