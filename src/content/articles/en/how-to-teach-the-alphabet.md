**In short:** introduce a few letters at a time, always with their **sound** and a picture your child already knows. Start with the letters in their name, play for five minutes a day, and save the pencil for after age 4. Kids don’t learn the alphabet from a chart; they learn it in everyday life.

## Letter names and letter sounds

Every letter has a **name** (“em”) and a **sound** (“mmm”, as in mouse). Reading needs the sound, so introduce both together: “This is M. It says mmm, like mouse and milk.” A picture helps a lot: in the app and on the worksheets on this site, each letter is a character, C for cat, S for sheep. Your child remembers the character and, with it, the letter.

English spelling is tricky: “sh”, “ch”, “th” and long vowels come later. At the start, stick to one sound per letter.

## What to do at each age

| Age | What usually works |
|---|---|
| 2–3 | sing the ABC song, look at picture books with big letters, point out the first letter of their name, make letters from play dough |
| 3–4 | recognize the letters in their name, spot letters on signs and cereal boxes, play “what sound does it start with?” |
| 4–5 | know the sound of most letters, start [letter tracing](/en/guides/how-to-teach-letter-tracing/) with a finger and then a pencil |
| 5–6 | blend sounds into short words (sat, pin, mom), read simple words, write their own name |

These are rough guides, not targets. Children of the same age can be at very different stages, and that’s fine.

## Which letters first?

1. **The letters in their name.** Your child sees them every day: on a backpack, on drawings, on the bedroom door.
2. **A first phonics set:** [S](/en/letter-tracing/letter-s/), [A](/en/letter-tracing/letter-a/), [T](/en/letter-tracing/letter-t/), [P](/en/letter-tracing/letter-p/), [I](/en/letter-tracing/letter-i/), [N](/en/letter-tracing/letter-n/). Many phonics programs start here, because these six letters already make lots of words: sat, tap, pin, nap, ant, sit.
3. **Next sounds:** m, d, g, o, c, k, e, u, r, h, b, f, l.
4. **The rest**, with less common letters like j, v, w, x, y, z, q at the end.

## One or two letters a week

A simple routine for each new letter:

- **Monday:** introduce the letter and its sound, look at its character, think of three words.
- **During the week:** hunt for the letter at home and outside, make it with your body, play dough or sticks.
- **End of the week:** trace the big letter with a finger and, from age 4, follow the dotted lines with a pencil.

On Saturday, review the letters from earlier weeks. Repetition is what makes them stick.

## Uppercase and lowercase

Capitals are easier to recognize and draw, so they often come first. But books are mostly lowercase, so show both forms early: “This is big A, this is little a.” Look-alike lowercase letters (b, d, p, q) need extra attention.

## Common mistakes adults make

- **Too many letters at once.** Two letters learned well beat ten mixed up.
- **Names only.** Without sounds, a child can recite the alphabet but can’t read a word.
- **Quizzing instead of playing.** “What letter is this?” ten times in a row gets old fast. Try these [alphabet games](/en/guides/alphabet-games/) instead.
- **Comparing with other kids.** The pace varies hugely before age 6.
